Budget and sandbox check

This check looks at what your design and your scripts will cost in your team’s lease, and at what the lease refuses. It is part of ① Design and requirement checks on the Checkpoint 2 page. Run it before you create anything in your lease, and again before each deploy. Your team has 30 USD of AWS for the term in its lease, and 5 USD for LLM calls through the course LLM gateway for now (20 USD for the term on request). The check is feedback for your team, not a grade: each Problem below says what you would see, the number that shows it, and what to do. Many of them are about an idle resource: one that costs money as long as it exists, even when nobody uses it.

The prices are from the course price sheet, 436c-student/prices.md on the course server (ca-central-1, USD), and the lease’s limits from 436c-student/resources.md on the course server. Each number below is one example, worked from the sheet for one size and one length of time; price your own design with its own sizes and hours.

BC005 Your team’s budget alert

Look in: your team’s answer (a budget is not in the repository), docs/design.md.

Problem: your team has no AWS budget with an email alert. Set one up once for your team’s lease: a cost budget of 30 USD for the whole term, with an email to every team member when actual spend passes 3, 5, 10, 15 and 20 USD (each person confirms the email from AWS before alerts arrive). AWS guide: Creating a cost budget. In the console: Billing and Cost Management → Budgets → Create budget → Customize (advanced) → Cost budget, period Custom from the first day of your lease to the end of term, then one alert for each amount (Absolute value, Actual). Or in CloudShell (ca-central-1), with your team’s emails:

ACCOUNT=$(aws sts get-caller-identity --query Account --output text)
EMAILS="first@student.ubc.ca second@student.ubc.ca third@student.ubc.ca"
cat > budget.json <<'JSON'
{"BudgetName": "team-term-budget", "BudgetType": "COST", "TimeUnit": "CUSTOM",
 "BudgetLimit": {"Amount": "30", "Unit": "USD"},
 "TimePeriod": {"Start": "2026-11-01T00:00:00Z", "End": "2026-12-31T00:00:00Z"}}
JSON
python3 - "$EMAILS" > notifications.json <<'PY'
import json, sys
subs = [{"SubscriptionType": "EMAIL", "Address": a} for a in sys.argv[1].split()]
print(json.dumps([{"Notification": {"NotificationType": "ACTUAL", "ComparisonOperator": "GREATER_THAN",
                                    "Threshold": t, "ThresholdType": "ABSOLUTE_VALUE"},
                   "Subscribers": subs} for t in (3, 5, 10, 15, 20)]))
PY
aws budgets create-budget --account-id "$ACCOUNT" --budget file://budget.json \
  --notifications-with-subscribers file://notifications.json
aws budgets describe-notifications-for-budget --account-id "$ACCOUNT" --budget-name team-term-budget \
  --query 'Notifications[].Threshold'

The last command should print the five amounts. AWS updates budget figures up to three times a day, usually 8 to 12 hours apart, so an alert can come hours after the spending: after each run, check that your resources are deleted instead of waiting for an alert. AWS guide: Managing your costs with AWS Budgets.

To see what is costing money: open Cost Explorer in the console (Billing and Cost Management → Cost Explorer) and show daily costs grouped by service; the console view is free. From the CLI, aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=<first of month>,End=<tomorrow> --granularity DAILY --metrics UnblendedCost --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=SERVICE gives the same figures: it only reads, but AWS charges for each Cost Explorer API request. AWS guide: Analyzing your costs with AWS Cost Explorer.

BC010 Things that cost money while nobody uses them (idle resources)

Look in: docs/design.md Resources, scripts/.

This question looks for idle resources in your design and your scripts.

Problem: your design uses an idle resource and your clean-up script does not delete it. Idle resources: EC2 instances, EMR clusters, Kinesis streams, load balancers, NAT gateways, RDS and Aurora databases, OpenSearch Serverless collections, MSK clusters, ElastiCache nodes, SageMaker endpoints, public IPv4 addresses. Three EMR nodes (m7g.xlarge) left on for a week cost about 112 USD; your team has 30 USD for the term. An ECS service on Fargate is not one by itself: Fargate charges for a task’s vCPU and memory while the task runs, so a service at zero tasks costs nothing, but its load balancer (about 4.16 USD a week) and a NAT gateway (8.40 a week) cost money as long as they exist. For each resource in your design, say what costs money (the instance, the nodes, the stream, the address, the storage) and how your clean-up script stops it after each run: create it for a run, then delete it. S3 storage and DynamoDB on-demand tables are not idle resources: they charge for what they store and for the requests they serve, so list them with what your team keeps between runs.

Problem: your design has an idle resource and does not say what your system costs in a month when nobody uses it. Add up every idle resource for 30 days. If the total is more than a few dollars, your design should create those resources only for runs and demos.

Problem: you chose a service whose smallest setting costs more than your budget. OpenSearch Serverless is charged in OpenSearch Compute Units (OCUs), units of indexing and search capacity: a collection needs OCUs for indexing and for search, at least 2, about 89 USD a week, unless its collection group can scale to zero. An MSK Serverless cluster costs about 141 USD a week while it exists. A SageMaker endpoint on ml.m5.large that runs all week costs about 21.50 USD. Aurora PostgreSQL Serverless v2 is charged in Aurora capacity units (ACUs) for every hour it runs: 23.52 USD a week for each ACU of its minimum capacity, plus storage. Price the setting your design names, for the hours it runs. For vectors use S3 Vectors or DynamoDB vector search; for queries Athena or DuckDB; for a small database RDS PostgreSQL db.t4g.micro (3.02 USD a week, plus storage). AWS guide: Working with S3 Vectors and vector buckets.

Problem: you plan a database server where a table would do. An RDS PostgreSQL database is an idle resource: db.t4g.micro (single-AZ) left on until the end of term (three weeks, 504 hours) costs about 9.07 USD, nearly a third of your budget, plus its storage at 0.127 USD per GB-month. If the app reads items by key, DynamoDB on demand charges for the requests (0.6875 USD per million write request units, 0.1375 per million read request units) and for storage beyond the first 25 GB (0.275 USD per GB-month), plus any index or backup you turn on; if it runs analysis queries, Athena or DuckDB over Parquet charges only for the queries. RDS is not yet tried in a student lease: check that you can create it before you depend on it.

Problem: a stopped EC2 instance still costs money. A stopped instance is no longer charged for itself, but its EBS volume is still charged (gp3, 0.088 USD per GB-month), and so is an Elastic IP address your team allocated (0.005 USD an hour, more than a t4g.nano). A public IPv4 address that AWS assigned at launch is released when the instance stops. Copy any data you need off the volume, then delete the volume and release the Elastic IP address. After you terminate an instance, list what is left: aws ec2 describe-volumes --filters Name=status,Values=available and aws ec2 describe-addresses. AWS guides: Public IPv4 addresses, Elastic IP addresses.

BC020 Costs that grow with each query and each run

Look in: docs/design.md (Data, Resources), docs/requirements.md (your long-term horizon RQ’s reply), scripts/, features/.

Problem: an Athena query reads the whole source data set instead of your subset. Athena charges for the data scanned: 5.50 USD per TB in ca-central-1. A source data set on S3 can be far larger than the course preview: measure it first (aws s3 ls --recursive --summarize s3://…). Query your own Parquet copy of your subset, with a partition filter. Athena guide: Partition your data.

Problem: your data is in CSV or JSON and every query reads all of it. Athena reads every column of a CSV or JSON file, even the ones the query does not use. Convert to Parquet once, partition by the column your queries filter on, and compare the data scanned before and after. Athena guide: Use columnar storage formats.

Problem: an EventBridge Scheduler schedule or rule starts a job or query more often than your users need, and each run has a minimum charge. An EventBridge Scheduler schedule or rule that runs every 15 minutes starts 2,880 runs a month. Athena charges at least 10 MB for each query (100,000 small queries cost about 5.50 USD). A Glue job is charged per DPU-hour (0.308 USD) with a one-minute minimum, and a Glue crawler at its own rate (0.44 USD per DPU-hour). EMR Serverless charges each worker’s vCPU and memory with a one-minute minimum per worker. An EMR cluster on EC2 is charged for every second it runs, the time between its steps included. Run it as often as your freshness requirement needs, no more.

Problem: an EventBridge Scheduler schedule, an EventBridge rule or a Glue trigger still exists after your clean-up script ran, and keeps starting work. An EventBridge Scheduler schedule, an EventBridge rule with a schedule expression (cron or rate), or an AWS Glue trigger of type SCHEDULED keeps starting a Glue or EMR job, a Step Functions workflow or a Lambda function, and that work can create resources again or keep writing data. A Glue job of 2 DPUs started every 15 minutes for a week is 672 runs, at least 6.90 USD at the one-minute minimum. Your clean-up script deletes these first: aws scheduler delete-schedule --name <name>; aws events remove-targets --rule <name> --ids <target ids>, then aws events delete-rule --name <name> (a rule that still has targets cannot be deleted); aws glue delete-trigger --name <name>. To list what is left: aws scheduler list-schedules, aws events list-rules --name-prefix <your prefix>, aws glue get-triggers. AWS guides: Deleting a schedule in EventBridge Scheduler, DeleteRule, Glue triggers.

Problem: the dashboard runs a query every time someone opens it. Compute the number the dashboard shows once, store it, and read the stored result.

Problem: your LLM calls can use up your team’s 5 USD before the demo. For each model, multiply the input tokens and the output tokens of one measured request by their own rates (through the course LLM gateway, gpt-6-luna is 0.29 CAD per million input tokens and 1.74 CAD per million output tokens), then by the requests you expect, plus each eval run: cases × repetitions, every time you run your tests or push to CI. Add the embedding calls, and compare the total with what is left of the pool (5 USD is 7.25 CAD). When the team’s pool is used up, the course LLM gateway refuses every call (error 429 team_budget_exhausted). Run evals on demand, not on every push; embed your documents once and reuse the vectors.

Problem: you load your history into DynamoDB one row at a time. 100 million writes of items up to 1 KB cost about 69 USD (68.75) in write request units, before any index writes and storage. An import from S3 into a new table costs 0.165 USD per GB of uncompressed source data (about 1.65 for 10 GB) and uses no write units. Or keep the history in S3 and put in DynamoDB only the items the app reads by key. DynamoDB guide: Requesting a table import in DynamoDB.

Problem: your functions log every record or every LLM reply. CloudWatch charges 0.55 USD per GB of logs received. Log counts, errors and a sample, and set a short retention on the log group. CloudWatch guide: Working with log groups and log streams.

Problem: your design moves data out of AWS or to another Region. On the price sheet, data sent to the internet costs 0.09 USD per GB (first 10 TB), and data sent to us-east-1 costs 0.02 USD per GB; another Region has its own rate. Keep the processing in ca-central-1 and send only the result the app shows.

Problem: your first run on AWS replays your whole simulated data set. Run a small sample first (1%, or one batch), check the cost, then scale up.

BC030 What your team’s lease refuses

Look in: docs/design.md Resources, scripts/, any instance type, Region or model your design names.

Problem: your design uses an EC2 instance type the lease does not allow. The lease allows t4g nano to large (no t4g.xlarge), m7g medium to xlarge, m8g.xlarge, and t3a and m6a.xlarge only when something does not run on ARM; other types are refused with UnauthorizedOperation. Test a type with aws ec2 run-instances --dry-run: DryRunOperation means it is allowed. AWS CLI reference: run-instances.

Problem: your design runs more than 10 Lambda invocations at the same time. The lease allows 10 concurrent invocations across all your functions together (the AWS default is 1,000): eleven at once of one function is already over. Above that, a synchronous caller (API Gateway, your script) gets a throttling error (429); an asynchronous invocation goes back to Lambda’s queue and is tried again later. Batch the work, or set reserved concurrency to limit how many run at once. Lambda guides: Event Function scaling behavior, Configuring reserved concurrency for an Event Function.

Problem: your design uses a Region other than ca-central-1, or a model that is not offered there. Everything runs in ca-central-1. Current Claude models are not offered on demand in ca-central-1; use the course LLM gateway.

Problem: your scripts assume a default VPC. The lease has one VPC with four subnets and no default; name a subnet in every launch. A Fargate task reaches the internet from a public subnet with a public IP; a NAT gateway is an idle resource.

Problem: your scripts call Service Quotas or the Pricing API. The lease refuses both. Read limits from the service’s own describe calls or the AWS documentation, and prices from the course price sheet.

Problem: your design creates an Athena view over the course’s shared tables. Athena refuses a view over a table in another account. Make the view over your own copy.

Problem: your team cannot find everything it created. The whole team shares one lease and one 30 USD budget. Give every resource the team’s name prefix and the course tags, so one list (the cost-and-teardown recipe’s inventory) finds what each member created. When the lease ends, everything in it is deleted: your scripts must be able to create the system again.

The recipe is in the course recipes repository: cost-and-teardown.

Run it

With the course server connected to your assistant, ask: “Run the budget and sandbox check on our repository.” It reads the files each question names under Look in, writes one file, docs/reviews/budget-check.md, and changes nothing else in your repository or in your lease. For BC005 it asks you whether your team has a budget.

By hand: go through each Problem above, look in the places its question names, and note each line of your design or scripts that it describes, with its file and line. The same check as plain files, as your assistant reads it, is in the repository mcp-static-content (signed in to github.students). Decide which of them to change before you deploy.

When you have checked your design, go back to the Checkpoint 2 page: ③ Implementation, a first slice on AWS.

After the check

Take one Problem at a time and fix what you choose. In docs/design.md Resources, replace the service, or say when it is created and deleted. Put the delete command for each resource in scripts/teardown.sh, and write down what your team keeps between runs (for example the data in S3). Then run the check again: a later run shows what is gone.

If your team decides not to change something this check lists, record it as a decision line, as Checkpoint 2, ① Design and requirement checks describes. The check keeps the item on its list with your reason beside it.

When to run it again

After you add a resource to your design, before each deploy, and when a budget alert arrives.
